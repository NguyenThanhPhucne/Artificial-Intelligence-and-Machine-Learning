I write for a security engineering lead who runs network monitoring on cloud infrastructure: someone fluent in networking (ports, TCP, flows) who must decide whether a machine-learning detector is worth deploying, but who is not a machine-learning specialist, so I explain results in terms of missed attacks, false alarms and running cost. Near-perfect scores are easy to obtain on this dataset for the wrong reasons: Liu et al. (2022) documented errors in how its attacks were run, its features generated and its flows labelled, and Arp et al. (2022) list data snooping, spurious correlations and lab-only evaluation among the most common pitfalls of machine learning in security. Each research question tests one of these risks. RQ1: once duplicate flows and the label-proxy fields (destination port, timestamp) are removed, how well do eight classifiers (Decision Tree, Random Forest, Gaussian Naive Bayes, k-Nearest Neighbours, Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine, Gradient Boosting and Multi-layer Perceptron) separate FTP and SSH brute-force flows from benign traffic, measured by cross-validated macro F1, per-class recall and running cost? RQ2: do the models recognise attack behaviour or testbed artefacts, measured by the change in macro F1 when the destination port is added and the attackers' fixed TCP settings are withheld, and by their predictions on a day they never saw (15 February 2018, with two unseen DoS attacks)? RQ3: with only 46 distinct FTP flows, does SMOTE improve rare-class detection compared with class weighting, Tomek-link under-sampling or no resampling?
