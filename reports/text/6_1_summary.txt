I built a reproducible pipeline that classifies network flows from one day of CSE-CIC-IDS2018 as Benign, FTP-BruteForce or SSH-Bruteforce, and compared eight classification algorithms under identical conditions. The most consequential step was data preparation: 99.98% of FTP-BruteForce rows were exact repeats, and the destination port and timestamp encode how the labels were made; without de-duplication 99.96% of FTP-BruteForce test flows would have been exact copies of training flows. On the cleaned data every algorithm separates both attacks almost perfectly (at most 3 errors in 15,000 test flows). For the three research questions: (RQ1) flow statistics alone separate these brute-force attacks from benign traffic for all eight algorithms; (RQ2) no model needs the destination port to reach its score, Gaussian Naive Bayes, k-Nearest Neighbours, Multi-layer Perceptron, Random Forest and Gradient Boosting lose 0.01 or more macro F1 without the attacking hosts' TCP settings, while Decision Tree, Logistic Regression and Support Vector Machine do not; on an unseen day the models raise 0–3 false alarms on 20,000 benign flows, but Decision Tree misses up to 99% of an unseen DoS attack, and the alarms it does raise disappear when the hosts' TCP settings are withheld, so even the most robust model partly recognises the attacking machines; (RQ3) SMOTE brought no consistent benefit, because the minority class was already separable. The pre-registered rule selects k-Nearest Neighbours; because the models differ by a handful of flows, cost, robustness and interpretability should decide deployment.

Recommended next steps for a security engineering lead: (1) treat these scores as an upper bound, since the data covers one day and two attacks, each launched from one Kali Linux machine; (2) the second-day check shows that false alarms stay rare on new benign traffic, but attacks of a new type are largely missed, so the detector must sit beside, not replace, signature-based detection, and should be validated on locally captured traffic with a time-based split before deployment; (3) deploy Decision Tree as a lightweight first-line filter in front of existing signature-based detection (0.60 ms per 1,000 flows), retraining when new attack types are labelled; (4) retrain it without the window sizes and Fwd Seg Size Min, which costs the Decision Tree no cross-validated accuracy (-0.0051 macro F1), so that its alarms describe attack behaviour rather than the machines used in this testbed.
