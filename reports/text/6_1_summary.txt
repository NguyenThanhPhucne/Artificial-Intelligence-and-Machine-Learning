I built a reproducible pipeline that classifies network flows from one day of CSE-CIC-IDS2018 as Benign, FTP-BruteForce or SSH-Bruteforce, and compared eight classification algorithms under identical conditions. The most consequential step was data preparation: 99.98% of FTP-BruteForce rows were exact repeats, and the destination port and timestamp encode how the labels were made; without de-duplication 99.96% of FTP-BruteForce test flows would have been exact copies of training flows. On the cleaned data every algorithm separates both attacks almost perfectly (at most 3 errors in 15,000 test flows). For the three research questions: (RQ1) flow statistics alone separate these brute-force attacks from benign traffic; (RQ2) 6 of 8 models gain from the destination-port shortcut, and Gaussian Naive Bayes, k-Nearest Neighbours, Random Forest, Logistic Regression and Gradient Boosting also lean on attacker-specific TCP values, while Decision Tree, Support Vector Machine and Multi-layer Perceptron do not; (RQ3) SMOTE brought no consistent benefit, because the minority class was already separable. The pre-registered rule selects k-Nearest Neighbours; because the models differ by a handful of flows, cost, robustness and interpretability should decide deployment.

Recommended next steps for a security engineering lead: (1) treat these scores as an upper bound, since the data covers one day and two attacks, each launched from one Kali Linux machine; (2) before deployment, validate on other CSE-CIC-IDS2018 days and on locally captured traffic, using a time-based split; (3) deploy Decision Tree as a lightweight first-line filter in front of existing signature-based detection (0.60 ms per 1,000 flows), retraining when new attack types are labelled; (4) monitor the false-alarm rate, the error that dominated here.
