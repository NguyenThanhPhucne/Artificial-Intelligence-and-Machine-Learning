I built a reproducible pipeline that classifies network flows from one day of CSE-CIC-IDS2018 as Benign, FTP-BruteForce or SSH-Bruteforce. The most consequential step was data preparation: 99.98% of FTP-BruteForce rows were exact repeats, and the destination port and timestamp encode how the labels were made; without de-duplication 99.96% of FTP-BruteForce test flows would have been exact copies of training flows. On the cleaned data all three algorithms detect both attacks almost perfectly: the selected Decision Tree detects all 9 FTP-BruteForce test flows with test accuracy 0.9999, trains in 0.45 s on 60,000 flows and classifies 1,000 flows in 0.58 ms. For the three research questions: (RQ1) flow statistics alone separate these brute-force attacks from benign traffic, with at most 3 errors in 15,000 test flows; (RQ2) every model gains from the destination-port shortcut, and Logistic Regression and Random Forest also lean on attacker-specific TCP values (macro F1 falls by up to 0.057 without them), while Decision Tree does not; (RQ3) SMOTE brought no benefit, because the minority class was already separable. Because the differences between models amount to one or two flows, the choice should rest on cost and interpretability rather than on accuracy.

Recommended next steps for a security engineering lead: (1) treat these scores as an upper bound, since the data covers one day and two attacks, each launched from one Kali Linux machine; (2) before deployment, validate on other CSE-CIC-IDS2018 days and on locally captured traffic, using a time-based split; (3) deploy the Decision Tree as a lightweight first-line filter in front of existing signature-based detection, retraining when new attack types are labelled; (4) monitor the false-alarm rate, the error that dominated here.
