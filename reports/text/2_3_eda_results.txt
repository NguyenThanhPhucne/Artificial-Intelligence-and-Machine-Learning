Class imbalance (Q1). The raw file looks moderately balanced (FTP-BruteForce 18.4%, SSH-Bruteforce 17.9%), but 99.98% of the FTP rows were exact repeats. After de-duplication the training set is severely imbalanced: Benign 84.76%, SSH-Bruteforce 15.18% and FTP-BruteForce 0.06% (1,375 benign flows per FTP flow; Figure 2.1). A classifier that never predicts FTP-BruteForce would lose only 0.06 percentage points of accuracy, so accuracy alone cannot reveal whether the rare attack is detected.

Scale and redundancy (Q3). The 66 non-constant numeric features range from binary flag counts to flow durations of up to 119,999,221 and byte rates of up to 394,000,000. After a symmetric log transform the highest-variance features are timing statistics (inter-arrival and idle times). Among the 20 features in Figure 2.2, 9 of the 190 pairs have |r| ≥ 0.95, for example Idle Max / Idle Mean (r = 1.000); Bwd IAT Tot / Bwd IAT Max (r = 0.998); Fwd IAT Tot / Fwd IAT Max (r = 0.995); in addition, Pkt Len Var equals Pkt Len Std squared on every flow.

Discriminative features (Q2). Mutual information with the label shows that many single features almost determine the class: 26 of 66 features reach at least 90% of the label entropy H(Y) = 0.431 nats, led by Fwd Pkt Len Max (98.8%). Figure 2.3 explains why. FTP-BruteForce flows carry no payload (100% have zero bytes in both directions), and SSH-Bruteforce flows are almost identical to one another (99.8% share the same maximum forward packet length of 640 bytes), so their boxes collapse to a line, whereas benign traffic spreads over several orders of magnitude. Both attacks also share an initial forward TCP window of 26,883 bytes (100% of FTP and 99.78% of SSH flows, against 0.065% of benign flows). This value most likely reflects the attacker's operating system rather than the attack itself, so a model that relies on it may learn to recognise the attacking machine instead of brute-force behaviour.

Outliers (Q3). Within each class, Tukey's 1.5 × IQR rule flags up to 22.7% of benign flows (Init Bwd Win Byts) and at most 6.3% of attack flows on the six features shown. These points are legitimate heavy tails of real traffic (large transfers, long sessions), not recording errors, so I keep them: removing them would make benign traffic look more uniform than it is.

Shortcut check (Q2). In the training set 100% of FTP flows use port 21 and 100% of SSH flows use port 22, against 0.006% and 0.079% of benign flows. Because CIC derived the labels from ports and the attack schedule, the port would let a model reproduce the labelling rule rather than learn traffic behaviour.
